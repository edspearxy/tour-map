## 3.1 Job-to-be-done strength: 10 / 15

**Justification:** The brief states the job separately from the solution, and the job is specific. It also says why the job is painful. Frequency and pain rest on one person's experience, and nothing in the research backs them up. So the job is real but could be a nice-to-have. That puts it on the 10 anchor, not the 15.

What earns the 10 (the job is named, and it is not just the feature restated):
> "A list answers 'when is the next show?' but not the question a fan actually has: **'where in the country can I see them, and which date is realistically reachable?'** — for themselves, or for someone they're buying tickets for."

> "Answering that from a list means reading every entry and placing each town on a mental map."

Why it does not reach 15 (frequency and pain are not established beyond n=1):
> "Personal frustration: I've repeatedly read down long tour lists trying to work out which dates are reachable, and the existing map option is awkward to use."

- The research has nothing that corroborates the pain. On the alternative that actually competes, it says: "No sourced evidence found that singles out Bandsintown's artist-page map as hard to use… The product owner's first-hand view is therefore not corroborated or contradicted by any published source found."
- On map vs list, the research says: "no sourced evidence found. The search returned no study comparing the two."
- The second segment, gift buyers "for someone who lives elsewhere", is only partly supported. The research has "YouGov (Feb 2023): 10% of respondents bought tickets as presents" but also "Share of gig tickets bought for someone in a different city: no sourced evidence found." The "lives elsewhere" part is the bit that makes geography matter to a gift buyer, and it is the unsupported part.
- How often the job comes up is never discussed. A fan checks a given artist's UK tour a few times a year, and a UK tour is often a list of 5 to 20 dates. The brief does not say why reading that list hurts enough to make someone go to a different site.

## 3.2 Value differentiation vs. status quo: 7 / 10

**Justification:** The brief names both real alternatives: doing nothing (reading the list) and Bandsintown's map. It argues against each one directly. But the only reason to switch is a UX hypothesis that nobody has tested. The brief's own data source weakens it against the list, and the brief itself concedes that users will not switch. Since the score sits between 7 and 10, I took the lower anchor.

Addressed against the status quo (why this is above 3):
> "A website where **the map is the page**… rather than a list with a map tucked behind it."

> "Bandsintown already offers an event map on its artist pages, but the map is secondary to the list, sits inside a wider discovery product, and is not the thing you land on. From first-hand use, its map is **hard to navigate**"

Why it falls short of 10:
1. **Against "read the list", the switch can make the answer worse.** The list on an artist's site is complete. This product is not: "only dates Ticketmaster sells or lists appear, so tours sold mainly through other ticket agents (See Tickets, Dice, AXS) will show gaps." The research shows the gaps could be large: "Ticketmaster's own submission says it partners with five of twelve major UK arenas; the other seven sell through other providers." The job is "which date is realistically reachable". If the reachable date is missing, the user gets the wrong answer faster. The brief never argues why a fan should trust a map that may be incomplete over a list that is complete.
2. **Against Bandsintown, the reason rests on one person's report.** "Hard to navigate" and "not the thing you land on" are both first-hand only. The research did not view the Bandsintown map ("The map was not viewed first-hand in this session") and found no evidence on its default framing.
3. **The brief concedes that users will not switch.** It says: "The product exists to test those interaction ideas, not to compete with Bandsintown for users" and "Bandsintown covers most of the job; this is unlikely to win users from it." That is honest for a learning project, but 3.2 asks why the target user would switch. Here the brief's own answer is that mostly they won't.
4. Other single-artist tour maps exist and are named in the research: Tour Radius ("search any artist to see the full tour"), MusicMap and Concert Map. They are part of what a fan might use today, and the brief does not mention them. They are unverified as live, so I have only noted this and not marked down further for it.

## Substance gate (User-Value lens): 4 / 10

Sampled items, each traced through the job and the switching argument:

**Trace 1: "its map is hard to navigate" (the basis for the switching argument against Bandsintown).**
The brief openly labels this as first-hand ("From first-hand use"), so it does not overclaim. The research finds nothing for or against it. The claim is attributed honestly but unsupported, and it is the only thing that sets this product apart from the closest alternative. The validation path does test it directly ("asking them to find a given date on this map and on Bandsintown's"). **This trace holds as a hypothesis with a test attached.** It is not yet supported.

**Trace 2: data gaps are "acceptable for an experiment, and visible to the user."**
This does not hold up:
- The brief describes no way to make gaps visible. The Discovery API returns only the events Ticketmaster lists. Nothing in the brief or the research shows how the product would know which dates of a tour are missing, so it cannot show the user that a date is missing.
- The brief's own Risks section contradicts it: "some tours will be incomplete, which could make the map look wrong rather than just sparse." A gap the user cannot see is one they read as "no show there". For this job, that is the worst failure: the user concludes no reachable date exists when one does.
- The research suggests the gaps will be big, not marginal: Ticketmaster partners with "five of twelve major UK arenas", and "tour-date completeness versus artists' own listings: no sourced evidence found."
- **Result:** the claim appears in the brief but has no support, and the brief contradicts it elsewhere. That matches the 4 anchor.

**Trace 3 (spot check): gift buyers "for someone who lives elsewhere."**
The research supports only the general gifting figure (10%). It explicitly finds no data on the "different city" share. So this segment is named but not supported. It does not change the score, but it is the second item that fails to trace.

**Falsification: the strongest case that this idea is wrong for its user:**
The user's question is "which date is realistically reachable?" Answering it needs the complete set of dates. The artist's own list has that. This product shows a subset, perhaps less than half for arena tours. So the map-first view answers the job faster but less accurately than the status quo. The Bandsintown comparison in "What success looks like" ("people find this one quicker and easier to navigate") is also confounded: if Bandsintown shows more dates, a speed test measures less clutter as much as better UX.

**Does the brief anticipate this?** Partly. The validation path says: "Check early how many of those tours Ticketmaster covers in full — if most have large gaps, the data source, not the map, is the constraint." That is a real, cheap check, and the brief deserves credit for it. But the brief also calls the gaps "visible to the user" without a mechanism, which papers over the problem. It never says what the user sees when their nearest date is missing, and it does not separate data completeness from UX in the success test.

**Substance score: 4 / 10.** One sampled item, the "visible to the user" claim at the centre of whether the product answers the job, is not supported and is contradicted by the brief's own Risks section. A second item, the gift-buyer "lives elsewhere" segment, is also unsupported by the research.

> Substance gate (User-Value lens): 4/10. At or below 4, so this caps the verdict at Revise by itself. Sampled trace failed: the brief says data gaps are "visible to the user" but gives no way to show them, and its own Risks section says incomplete tours "could make the map look wrong rather than just sparse".

### What would move this lens (for the author, not a verdict)
- Say how a missing date becomes visible. For example, show "Ticketmaster-listed dates only — check the artist's site for the full tour", or compare against a second source. Otherwise drop the "visible to the user" claim and own the risk.
- In the friends test, use tours where Ticketmaster coverage is complete, or count the dates on each map, so the result measures map UX rather than how many dates are shown.
- Either support the gift-buyer "lives elsewhere" segment with evidence or drop it.
