## 3.1 Job-to-be-done strength — 10 / 15

**Justification:** The job is real, specific and stated separately from the solution. The pain is only the author's own experience, and nothing shows how often it happens. A fan looks at a tour list now and then, when a tour is announced. That makes this occasional, not recurring. The brief doesn't show why it hurts enough that someone would change what they do.

What earns the 10 (job named and specific, stated before the solution):
> "A list answers 'when and where is the next show?' but not the question most fans actually have: **'is there a show I can realistically get to?'**"

> "To answer that from a list, a fan has to read every entry, recognise each town, and judge in their head how far it is from them."

Why it stops short of 15 (pain and frequency are asserted, not shown):
> "Personal frustration: I've repeatedly hit this myself — reading down a long tour list trying to work out which dates are realistically reachable"

That is one person's account. "The question most fans actually have" is a claim with no source. The market research has no evidence on how often fans hit this, or how much it bothers them.

The gift-buyer job is weaker. The brief calls it "the case where list views fail worst". The research says:
> "No sourced evidence found on the share of gig tickets bought for someone in a different city."
> "YouGov (February 2023): 10% of respondents said they bought tickets as presents"

That 10% covers all ticket gifting. It doesn't measure gifting for someone in another city, so the brief's strongest pain case has no evidence behind it.

There is also a scope problem inside the brief. The conditions that make the job hard are "the tour has many dates, or spans countries" and "foreign legs". The first version is "UK venues first". A UK fan reading a UK tour list mostly knows the towns. So the first version leaves out the situations where the job hurts most.

Between 15 and 10, scored down → **10**.

## 3.2 Value differentiation vs. status quo — 7 / 10

**Justification:** There is a believable reason to switch away from reading a plain list ("do nothing"). The switching argument against the named competitors relies on a description of them that the market research contradicts. Measured against what actually exists, the argument isn't made.

What earns points (a switching argument against the list and doing nothing):
> "To answer that from a list, a fan has to read every entry, recognise each town, and judge in their head how far it is from them."
> "A fan can go from 'artist's tour page' to 'the nearest date to this location, with a link to buy' in under a minute, without reading the full list."

The research confirms that the list really is the status quo:
> "Do nothing / read the list on the artist's own page. Tour pages are commonly published through embedded widgets, so the list view is the status quo"

What the research contradicts. The brief describes the competitors as location-first products that "simply show nothing" when an artist isn't playing nearby:
> "Existing live-event discovery products (Bandsintown, Songkick) are built around 'what's on near me?' ... This idea inverts it: start with who you want to see, then work out visually where you can see them."
> "Where an artist isn't playing anywhere local, the list-and-location products simply show nothing, while the map still shows the nearest options worth travelling for."

Market research:
> "Bandsintown artist-page event map - Artist pages gained an event map (powered by Stay22) showing all upcoming shows. Fans can view the whole tour or select dates to see shows in a range"
> "Bandsintown artist pages and app - list upcoming shows, show whether there are dates in the viewer's city"
> "Tour Radius - ... an interactive map where you search any artist to explore their full tour"

Bandsintown's own artist pages already start from the artist and already show the whole tour on a map. Several other single-artist tour maps exist too (Tour Radius, MusicMap, Concert Map, Artist Map). The brief's "inversion" is how the main existing product already works, so the switching argument has been made against a status quo that doesn't exist.

What might still set this apart is distance from any location, typed in by the user. That is the gift-buyer case. The research found "no sourced evidence found for or against" this on Bandsintown. The brief never presents this as the reason to switch from Bandsintown's artist map, because it doesn't know that map exists.

Two further points:
- **Paste-a-URL adds friction.** Bandsintown and Tour Radius let you search by artist name. Tour Map asks the user to find and paste a tour-page URL first. The brief never weighs that extra step against "under a minute".
- **The gift buyer has another option.** The research names "Gift vouchers instead of choosing a date ... let the recipient choose show and date". The brief doesn't mention it.

A believable reason to switch, not addressed against the real current alternative → **7**. This is generous. The rubric says a section that doesn't hold up against its source scores as missing, and the part comparing against competitors doesn't hold up. What survives is the comparison with "do nothing / read the list".

## Substance gate (User-Value lens): 4 / 10

**Trace 1 — "Where an artist isn't playing anywhere local, the list-and-location products simply show nothing" (brief, Positioning).**
This is the brief's main differentiation claim. The research directly contradicts it. Bandsintown artist pages "list upcoming shows" and include an "event map ... showing all upcoming shows", and they have done so since a 2022 changelog. The claim is stated in the brief but it doesn't hold up against the research. **Fails.**

**Trace 2 — the gift buyer as "the case where list views fail worst" (brief, Who has it).**
The brief uses this as its sharpest pain case. The research gives only a general 10% figure for ticket gifting and explicitly finds "No sourced evidence found on the share of gig tickets bought for someone in a different city." It also names a competing behaviour, vouchers, that the brief never engages. The claim is asserted, not supported. **Does not hold up.**

**Trace 3 (supporting) — the pain conditions vs. MVP scope.**
The brief says the job gets harder with tours that span countries and include foreign legs. The first version is UK-only. The first version leaves out the brief's strongest examples of the pain.

**Strongest argument that this is wrong (from this lens):**
The fans this is for already have a free tool that does most of the job. Search the artist on Bandsintown (or Apple Music, Maps or Shazam, which use Bandsintown data) and you get the whole tour, on a map, without finding and pasting a URL. What's left is distance from a location you type in, plus the remote gift buyer. That is a narrow slice, has no evidence behind it, and exists only on the author's own word. A Bandsintown map already gives a rough sense of distance at a glance. The brief doesn't anticipate this anywhere. Its positioning section is written as if single-artist maps didn't exist.

**Score: 4.** At least one sampled item is stated in the brief but contradicted by the market research. The strongest counter-argument isn't engaged.

**Context for synthesis (not a score change):** The brief frames this as "a side project scratching that itch ... there is no revenue target." So the bar for "someone would change behaviour" may matter less to this owner than to a commercial venture. The rubric still scores the user-value case as written.
