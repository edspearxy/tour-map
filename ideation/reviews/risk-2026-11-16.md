## 4.1 Named risks: **10 / 15**

**Justification:** The brief names technical risks (extraction, geocoding) and a regulatory/legal one (scraping terms), each specific to this idea. But it names no business or adoption risk at all, and the market research shows that gap is exactly where the biggest risk is.

**By category:**

- **Technical: named, with reasons.**
  > "**Extraction reliability:** tour pages vary widely in structure (plain HTML, embedded widgets, JavaScript-rendered lists). What share of real artist tour pages can be extracted accurately, and how is a failed or partial extraction shown to the user?"

  > "**Venue location accuracy:** turning a venue name and town into a correct map position, especially for small or ambiguously named venues."

  Both are specific to this product, not generic. The market research makes the extraction risk sharper: "Whether these widgets render client-side (JavaScript or iframe) was not established from the sources."
- **Regulatory/legal: named, but the reason is thin.**
  > "**Terms of use:** whether scraping a page on a user's behalf is acceptable under the source sites' terms."

  This is specific to the idea, but it is posed only as a question. The brief doesn't say what happens if the answer is no. The market research gives the concrete reason it matters, and the brief never links to it: "The Partner Search API page says not to scrape data or modify content" (Bandsintown), plus "Tour pages are commonly published through embedded widgets" (Bandsintown, Songkick Tourbox, Seated).
- **Business/adoption: not addressed.** The brief has no risk statement about adoption, discoverability or competitive displacement. The only commercial text, "a side project... there is no revenue target", explains why money risk is low. It doesn't name a risk. The brief's positioning goes further and *asserts away* the competitive risk: "the list-and-location products simply show nothing, while the map still shows the nearest options worth travelling for". The market research contradicts this: "Bandsintown artist-page event map: Artist pages gained an event map (powered by Stay22) showing all upcoming shows... Changelog dated 2022". It also lists Tour Radius ("search any artist to explore their full tour"), MusicMap, Concert Map and Artist Map. The risk that the core job is already served by the status-quo product most fans use is never named.

Under the anchor ("at least one category... is unaddressed"), this scores 10. It cannot get 15 because there is no business/adoption risk.

## 4.2 Reversibility / cheap validation path: **7 / 10**

**Justification:** The open questions show the author knows the extraction hit rate is unknown and must be learned. But the brief proposes no concrete, cheap step to learn it, or to test demand, before building.

> "What share of real artist tour pages can be extracted accurately, and how is a failed or partial extraction shown to the user?"

> "Drafted from the product owner's description and answers; the remaining open questions are listed at the end deliberately."

These earn 7: validation is recognised, but no step is specified. A cheap first step would have been easy to state, for example: hand-check 20–30 UK artist tour pages for structure and widget type, or test whether the existing Bandsintown artist-page map already answers the gift-buyer question. Neither is proposed. "Rough shape of the idea" goes straight to the built product ("Paste the URL... The dates are extracted... plotted as a pin"). Reversibility is inherently good, since this is a solo side project with "no revenue target". But the rubric scores the validation path the brief proposes, not cost that is low by circumstance. So it stays at 7, not 10.

## Substance gate (Risk lens): **4 / 10**

Scored last. I followed two sampled items through the risk-relevant parts of the brief, then wrote a falsification argument.

**Trace 1: Data approach as de-risking.**
Brief: "The MVP scrapes the tour list from a URL the user provides — **no dependency on a third-party events API or its licence**."
Against the market research, this does not hold. The research says artist tour pages are "commonly published through embedded widgets": Bandsintown widget, Songkick Tourbox, and Seated "on 894 domains". So scraping the artist's own page will often mean scraping a third-party widget's content. The licence dependency is moved, not removed. Bandsintown's own page "says not to scrape data or modify content". The brief does raise terms of use as an open question. But its data-approach section presents scraping as the way to *avoid* licence exposure, which contradicts its own source material. The "longer-term" API direction also has an unflagged constraint: "Each API key is tied to a single artist unless Bandsintown authorises otherwise". **Does not hold up.**

**Trace 2: Positioning claim that incumbents "simply show nothing".**
Brief: "Existing live-event discovery products (Bandsintown, Songkick) are built around 'what's on near me?'... Where an artist isn't playing anywhere local, the list-and-location products simply show nothing."
The market research directly contradicts the Bandsintown half: an artist-page event map "showing all upcoming shows. Fans can view the whole tour or select dates". It also lists several single-artist tour-map products. The research found "no sourced evidence found for or against" a distance-from-arbitrary-location feature on Bandsintown. The narrower differentiator (distance ranking from someone else's location, for gift buyers) may therefore survive. But the brief does not make that narrower claim, and it does not name the overlap as a risk. **Does not hold up as written.**

**Strongest falsification argument:**
On a large share of UK artist tour pages, the dates come from a Bandsintown, Songkick or Seated widget. Such widgets may be client-rendered (unverified) and are covered by terms that say not to scrape. For those pages the MVP either fails to extract the dates or extracts them against the provider's terms. Meanwhile, the same provider already gives fans an artist-level tour map, so the user who fails to get a result has a free fallback. The tool's remaining value then rests on the "distance from someone else's location" feature for gift buyers. The research found no sourced evidence on how often that need occurs ("No sourced evidence found on the share of gig tickets bought for someone in a different city"; ticket gifting overall is 10% of respondents).
The brief anticipates *parts* of this argument: extraction reliability and terms of use appear as open questions. It does not answer them, it does not connect them to the widget evidence, and it does not engage the competitor-overlap half at all.

**Score: 4.** Both sampled items are named in the brief, but neither is supported by the market research, and both contradict it. Per the rubric, a score of ≤ 4 triggers the Substance gate cap. Applying it is for synthesis.
